0 · Purpose, Scope & Disclaimer

This algorithm is designed for use by a consultant geriatrician (or trainee under supervision) assessing an older adult with a suspected neurocognitive disorder (NCD) in outpatient, inpatient consultation, or memory-clinic settings. It aims to:

  • Operationalise DSM-5-TR criteria for Major and Mild Neurocognitive Disorder into a stepwise clinical pathway.
  • Define the minimum basic assessment every patient should undergo before a diagnosis is finalised.
  • Explicitly flag atypical, young-onset, rapidly progressive, and diagnostic-overlap presentations that are easily missed if an amnestic-AD-first heuristic is applied by default.
  • Indicate when and why to extend beyond basic work-up to second-line biomarker, neurophysiological, CSF, genetic, or nuclear-medicine investigations.

Disclaimer: this document is a structured synthesis of DSM-5-TR criteria, Australian and international dementia guidelines, and consensus aetiological criteria, intended as a professional reference/checklist. It does not constitute a clinical practice guideline in its own right and should be applied with clinical judgement, updated against current local protocols and PBS/MBS criteria, and adapted to institutional pathways before adoption into routine practice.

1 · Presentation & Initial Clinical Evaluation

1.1 Common triggers for assessment

  • Self- or informant-reported cognitive complaint
  • Functional decline noted by family/carers or aged-care services
  • Behavioural, personality, or psychiatric change in an older adult
  • Incidental finding on hospital admission (e.g. medication error, falls, delirium)
  • Pre-operative or capacity/driving assessment referral
  • Screening in a patient with a strong risk profile (vascular disease, Parkinson disease, prior TBI, family history)

1.2 History — mandatory elements

DSM-5-TR requires evidence of decline from a previous level of functioning; history from a reliable informant is therefore not optional — it is part of Criterion A.

  • History from the patient AND a collateral historian who knows the patient well (essential — do not diagnose on patient self-report alone)
  • Onset and course: insidious/gradual (neurodegenerative) vs stepwise (vascular) vs subacute/rapid (days–weeks: delirium, autoimmune, prion) vs fluctuating (DLB, delirium)
  • Domain(s) first affected: memory, language, executive function, visuospatial, social cognition/behaviour
  • Functional trajectory: instrumental ADLs (finances, medications, driving, shopping) before basic ADLs
  • Full medication review — anticholinergic/sedative burden, recent medication changes
  • Alcohol and substance use history
  • Mood, anxiety, psychotic symptoms, apathy; prior psychiatric history
  • Sleep history — REM sleep behaviour disorder (dream enactment), snoring/apnoea, hypersomnolence, insomnia
  • Vascular risk factors and cerebrovascular disease history
  • Head injury history (including repetitive/sport-related)
  • Family history of dementia, Parkinsonism, motor neurone disease, or early-onset cognitive decline (ask age of onset in relatives)
  • Occupational/toxin exposure
  • HIV risk factors; travel/exposure history relevant to prion or infectious causes
  • Malignancy history (paraneoplastic risk)
  • Sensory function — hearing and vision (uncorrected impairment confounds cognitive testing)
  • Cultural, linguistic, and educational background (determines choice of cognitive instrument)

1.3 Exclude delirium before proceeding

Gate-keeping step. Screen with 4AT (or CAM/CAM-ICU in hospital) at first contact. If delirium is present or cannot be excluded: identify and treat the precipitant; DSM-5-TR Criterion C ("not occurring exclusively during delirium") means NCD severity/aetiology should not be finalised until delirium has resolved — reassess cognition after resolution (typically several weeks; longer in frail/older patients). Delirium superimposed on dementia is common — a pre-existing NCD diagnosis does not exclude a new delirium; treat first, then reassess.

What counts as a positive delirium screen — apply the tool's own scoring algorithm, not a vague impression:

ToolItems scoredPositive result
4AT(1) Alertness; (2) AMT4 — age, date of birth, place, current year; (3) Attention — months of the year backwards; (4) Acute change or fluctuating course over the last 2 weeks, still evident in the last 24 hours. 1.5 Distinguishing a functional cognitive disorder from neurodegenerative disease

Functional (non-progressive) cognitive disorder is an increasingly common differential, particularly in younger-old patients presenting with the "worried well" pattern. Consider it, rather than early dementia, when the following are present:

  • Internal inconsistency — able to perform a task well at times but fails when it becomes the focus of attention
  • A detailed, organised, or even over-inclusive account of memory lapses, with eloquent attribution to specific past events
  • Patient is more concerned about their cognitive function than a partner, family member, or colleague — and often attends alone
  • Obsessional personality traits, tendency to over-interpret normal cognitive lapses
  • Marked inconsistency between disastrous formal test scores and evidently intact day-to-day competence
  • Wavering concentration, "thought-blocking", information that was briefly forgotten soon re-emerges unprompted

Contrast this with Alzheimer disease, where patients typically minimise or "face-save" around deficits, or with diseases where reduced insight leads to indifference. Do not dismiss the possibility of an emerging organic process, however — particularly in older patients, functional-type cognitive complaints can herald neurodegeneration and warrant follow-up rather than reassurance alone.

What ultimately separates MCI (Mild NCD) from dementia (Major NCD) is functional independence, not the test score, under DSM-5-TR Criterion B: a low score with preserved independence (more effort/compensation only) = Mild NCD; a similar score accompanied by genuine need for assistance with IADLs = Major NCD. Use the score bands above to raise or lower suspicion, then confirm severity against §2.2 functional assessment.

Formal neuropsychological testing (the reference standard when the distinction is unclear): under the commonly used Petersen/NIA-AA operational convention, performance roughly 1–1.5 SD below age- and education-adjusted normative means on formal testing, in one or more domains, with preserved daily functional independence, supports MCI (Mild NCD). More severe impairment (commonly >1.5–2 SD below norms), often across multiple domains, combined with functional dependency, supports dementia (Major NCD). Exact SD thresholds vary between diagnostic frameworks (e.g. Jak/Bondi actuarial neuropsychological criteria require impairment on ≥2 tests within a domain, each >1 SD below norms) — treat these as a guide, not a rigid rule, and lean on a neuropsychologist's interpretation where available.

The behavioural, mood, caregiver, and delirium tools in §2.3 and §1.3 (NPI-Q, GDS-15, Zarit, 4AT, CAM) are not cognitive-severity measures and do not have an MCI-vs-dementia score threshold — they characterise behavioural disturbance, mood, caregiver strain, and delirium respectively, each assessed independently of NCD severity staging.

2.2 Functional assessment

  • Instrumental ADLs — Lawton IADL scale (8 domains: telephone use, shopping, food preparation, housekeeping, laundry, transport, medication management, finances). Operational definition of impairment: needs assistance with, or cannot perform, ≥1 domain that the person previously managed independently — this is the functional threshold that separates Mild NCD (independence preserved, more effort/compensation needed) from Major NCD (assistance genuinely required)
  • Basic ADLs — Katz Index (6 domains: bathing, dressing, toileting, transferring, continence, feeding) or Barthel Index (0–100, 10 weighted domains). Operational definition: any dependency in a basic ADL domain establishes at least moderate-severity Major NCD; full dependence across domains = severe
  • Driving safety and financial/medical decision-making capacity screen — any identified impairment triggers the safety actions in §9, independent of formal NCD staging

2.3 Behavioural, mood & caregiver assessment

  • NPI-Q (Neuropsychiatric Inventory Questionnaire) — 12 domains rated present/absent by the informant; if present, severity (1 = mild to 3 = severe) and caregiver distress (0–5) are rated. "Positive" = any domain endorsed as present — there is no single pass/fail cut-off; use the pattern and severity to populate the DSM-5-TR behavioural-disturbance specifier (e.g. with agitation, with psychotic disturbance)
  • GDS-15 (Geriatric Depression Scale, 15-item) — score 0–15; ≥5 is suggestive of depression and warrants further assessment, ≥10 is highly suggestive of a depressive disorder. Use the Cornell Scale for Depression in Dementia (informant + observation based) once NCD is established and self-report is unreliable
  • 4AT / CAM — delirium screen; see §1.3 for the exact positive-result definitions. Repeat with any clinical fluctuation
  • Zarit Burden Interview — caregiver strain. On the original 22-item version (0–88): <21 little/no burden, 21–40 mild-to-moderate, 41–60 moderate-to-severe, >60 severe burden. If using an abbreviated (e.g. 12-item) version, its published cut-points differ by validation study — use the cut-off validated for the version and population in use; treat a materially elevated score as the trigger for escalating carer support regardless of the exact threshold

2.4 Physical & neurological examination

  • General examination — cardiovascular, thyroid, signs of systemic illness or malignancy
  • Gait and balance assessment
  • Parkinsonian signs — bradykinesia, rigidity, rest tremor, postural instability
  • Focal neurological signs — pyramidal signs, visual field deficits, apraxia, primitive/frontal release reflexes
  • Eye movements (vertical gaze palsy → consider PSP)
  • Myoclonus (→ consider CJD, advanced AD, metabolic cause)
  • Orthostatic blood pressure (autonomic dysfunction → consider DLB/MSA)

2.5 Basic laboratory investigations (all patients)

  • Full blood count
  • Electrolytes, urea, creatinine, calcium, glucose
  • Liver function tests
  • Thyroid function tests (TSH ± free T4)
  • Serum vitamin B12 and folate
  • Syphilis serology and HIV serology — if risk factors present (not routine in all guidelines, but low threshold in atypical/young-onset presentations)

2.6 Baseline structural neuroimaging

MRI brain (preferred) or CT brain (if MRI contraindicated/unavailable) should be performed in most patients to exclude structural lesions (tumour, subdural haematoma, normal-pressure hydrocephalus, significant cerebrovascular disease) and support aetiological subtyping. May be deferred only when the diagnosis is already clear in advanced, longstanding, typical dementia and imaging would not change management — use clinical judgement.

3.1 Specifiers to apply once Major or Mild NCD is established

  • Aetiological subtype (see §4) — e.g. "due to Alzheimer disease", "due to Lewy body disease", "due to multiple aetiologies", or "etiology unknown/unspecified" (DSM-5-TR 2022 addition) if work-up is incomplete or inconclusive
  • Behavioural disturbance specifier — "with" or "without" behavioural or psychological disturbance; for Major NCD, specify: with agitation, with anxiety, with mood symptoms, with psychotic disturbance, with other behavioural/psychological disturbance
  • Severity (Major NCD only) — Mild (difficulty with IADLs, e.g. housework, managing money), Moderate (difficulty with basic ADLs, e.g. feeding, dressing), Severe (fully dependent)

7 · Excluding Mimics & Reversible Contributors

Before finalising the diagnosis, actively exclude or quantify the contribution of the following, several of which are already captured by §1–2 but are re-listed here as a final checklist:

  • Functional cognitive disorder — internal inconsistency, disproportionate concern relative to informant, well-preserved daily function despite poor test performance (see §1.5); manage supportively but keep under review, as functional-type presentations can also herald neurodegeneration in older patients
  • Depression / "pseudodementia" — GDS-15 or Cornell Scale; consider a treatment trial where mood disorder is prominent, but note depression can also be a prodromal feature of neurodegeneration — reassess cognition after treatment rather than assuming full reversibility
  • Delirium (acute or superimposed on existing NCD) — 4AT/CAM; treat precipitant, defer final staging
  • Medication effects and polypharmacy — review anticholinergic burden, sedatives, opioids, recent changes
  • Alcohol and substance use
  • Metabolic/endocrine causes — thyroid dysfunction, B12/folate deficiency, renal/hepatic impairment, hypercalcaemia, hypoglycaemia (basic bloods, §2.5)
  • Uncorrected sensory impairment — hearing and vision — can mimic or exaggerate cognitive deficits on testing
  • Obstructive sleep apnoea and other sleep disorders contributing to daytime cognitive complaints
  • Normal pressure hydrocephalus — gait/incontinence/cognitive triad with disproportionate ventriculomegaly
  • Structural lesion (tumour, subdural haematoma, chronic infection) — excluded by baseline imaging

8 · Diagnostic Formulation Template

Once §1–7 are complete, formulate the diagnosis using the following structure:

[Patient] presents with [Major / Mild] Neurocognitive Disorder due to [aetiology, or multiple aetiologies, or unspecified/unknown aetiology], [with / without] behavioural or psychological disturbance [specify if present], [mild / moderate / severe] severity (Major NCD only).

Basis: DSM-5-TR Criteria A–D met, supported by [cognitive test(s) and score(s)], [functional assessment findings — IADL/ADL], [collateral history summary], [neuroimaging findings], and [any extended investigation results].

Atypical features considered: [list red flags screened and addressed, or state "none identified on §5 screen"].

Differential/mimics excluded: [delirium, depression, medication effect, metabolic cause, sensory impairment, NPH, structural lesion — as applicable].

9 · Management, Referral & Follow-up Considerations

  • Discuss diagnosis and prognosis with patient and family/carer, tailored to capacity and preference for disclosure
  • Safety review — driving, medication self-management, home safety, falls risk
  • Capacity assessment and advance care planning — enduring power of attorney/guardianship, advance health directive, will
  • Caregiver support — act on Zarit Burden Interview findings; refer to carer support services
  • Pharmacological management — confirm current PBS authority criteria (sMMSE and specialist-confirmation requirements apply and are reviewed periodically) before initiating cholinesterase inhibitors/memantine; treat BPSD per NPI-Q findings using non-pharmacological strategies first. See the Dementia Medications Guide
  • Vascular risk factor optimisation where relevant
  • Specialist referral pathways — memory clinic, neurology, neuropsychiatry, young-onset dementia service, genetics service, or prion disease unit, as triggered by §5–6 findings
  • Plan a follow-up interval appropriate to trajectory — shorter for rapidly progressive or diagnostically uncertain presentations, routine (6–12 months) for stable typical presentations

Appendix A · Comparative Reference — Major Dementia Types

A single reference comparing the aetiological subtypes introduced in §4, covering typical clinical presentation, examination signs, investigation findings, treatment, and prognosis/natural history. Use alongside, not instead of, the formal consensus criteria cited in §4. Prognosis/survival figures are approximate, drawn from published cohort ranges rather than a single definitive source, and vary with age at diagnosis, comorbidity, and access to care — use as a guide for patient/family discussion, not a precise individual prediction.

Alzheimer disease (typical, amnestic)

Presentation
Insidious onset, gradual progression; earliest and most prominent domain is episodic memory (repeats questions, misplaces items, poor new learning); anosognosia common; usually >65y (sporadic), early-onset familial forms <65y (PSEN1/2, APP)
Exam
Normal general/neurological exam early; parietal signs (dyspraxia, dyscalculia, visuospatial difficulty) emerge later; primitive reflexes and myoclonus in advanced disease
Investigations
MRI: hippocampal/medial temporal atrophy disproportionate to age. FDG-PET: temporoparietal hypometabolism. CSF: low Aβ42, high total-tau/p-tau. Amyloid PET positive. APOE ε4 = risk marker, not diagnostic; PSEN1/2, APP testing if early-onset familial
Treatment
Cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) mild–moderate stage; memantine moderate–severe (can combine); non-pharmacological: cognitive stimulation, routine, carer education; anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies (e.g. lecanemab, donanemab) for early symptomatic, biomarker-confirmed disease where locally available/funded — check current access criteria; treat vascular comorbidity
Prognosis
Slowly progressive; average 8–10 years from diagnosis to death (range 3–20y), varying with age and comorbidity; gradual continuous multidomain decline to full dependency

Vascular dementia (vascular NCD)

Presentation
Onset temporally linked to stroke/TIA, or insidious with prominent executive dysfunction, slowed processing speed, apathy and mood change; course classically stepwise (abrupt decline + plateau) with macrovascular disease, more insidious with small-vessel disease
Exam
Focal neurological signs (weakness, sensory loss, hemianopia), gait disturbance (broad-based, small-stepped), pseudobulbar features (emotional lability, dysarthria, dysphagia), pyramidal signs
Investigations
MRI/CT: territorial/strategic infarct or extensive subcortical white-matter change. Vascular work-up: lipids, HbA1c, carotid Doppler, AF screen. Often absent AD biomarkers (or coexist if mixed)
Treatment
Aggressive vascular risk-factor control (BP, lipids, glucose, antiplatelet/anticoagulation as indicated, smoking cessation) is central; cholinesterase inhibitors/memantine have modest/inconsistent evidence, sometimes trialled if mixed pathology suspected; rehabilitation (physio/OT/speech) for focal deficits
Prognosis
Variable; classically stepwise with interval infarcts and increased risk of further vascular events driving mortality; often shorter survival than AD due to cardiovascular comorbidity; can plateau between events

Dementia with Lewy bodies

Presentation
Fluctuating cognition/attention, recurrent detailed visual hallucinations, REM sleep behaviour disorder often predating decline by years, spontaneous parkinsonism within ~1 year of cognitive onset
Exam
Parkinsonism (bradykinesia, rigidity ± tremor), postural instability, hypomimia, autonomic dysfunction (orthostatic hypotension, constipation, urinary symptoms), severe neuroleptic sensitivity if exposed to antipsychotics
Investigations
DAT-SPECT: reduced striatal dopamine transporter uptake (indicative biomarker). MIBG cardiac scintigraphy: reduced uptake. Polysomnography: REM sleep without atonia. MRI: relative preservation of medial temporal structures (contrast with AD). EEG: prominent posterior slow-wave activity with periodic fluctuation
Treatment
Rivastigmine first-line for cognition and neuropsychiatric symptoms; AVOID typical/first-generation antipsychotics (risk of severe, potentially fatal neuroleptic sensitivity) — use low-dose quetiapine/clozapine cautiously if unavoidable; levodopa for parkinsonism (often less responsive, may worsen hallucinations); manage orthostatic hypotension and RBD
Prognosis
Generally faster progression and shorter survival than AD (commonly quoted ≈5–8 years from diagnosis); early falls, hospitalisation and care dependency common; day-to-day fluctuation in function

Parkinson disease dementia

Presentation
Established PD, motor symptoms precede cognitive decline by ≥1y (distinguishes from DLB); executive/visuospatial impairment, fluctuating attention, hallucinations
Exam
Established parkinsonism (tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, postural instability); autonomic dysfunction, RBD
Investigations
Clinical diagnosis in PD context; DAT-SPECT already abnormal from underlying PD (not additionally discriminating); MRI mainly to exclude other pathology
Treatment
Rivastigmine best evidenced, specifically indicated for PDD in many jurisdictions; balance dopaminergic therapy (motor benefit vs exacerbating hallucinations/confusion); avoid anticholinergics (worsen cognition) and typical antipsychotics; quetiapine/clozapine for psychosis if needed
Prognosis
Progressive; dementia onset marks more advanced/aggressive PD, associated with increased mortality and nursing-home admission; survival post-PDD-diagnosis highly variable across cohorts — shorter trajectory than PD without dementia

Frontotemporal dementia — behavioural variant

Presentation
Early-onset (often 45–65y), progressive change in personality/behaviour/social conduct — disinhibition, apathy, loss of empathy, perseverative/compulsive behaviours, hyperorality/dietary change; episodic memory and visuospatial skills relatively preserved early
Exam
Frontal release/primitive reflexes; may show mild parkinsonism or motor neurone disease signs (fasciculations, weakness, bulbar signs) in FTD-ALS overlap; executive dysfunction on testing despite deceptively preserved memory-test performance
Investigations
MRI: frontal and/or anterior temporal atrophy (often asymmetric, sometimes right-predominant). FDG-PET: frontotemporal hypometabolism (helpful if MRI equivocal). Genetic testing (C9orf72, MAPT, GRN) — familial in up to ~30–40% of cases. CSF Alzheimer biomarkers typically negative. NCS/EMG if ALS overlap suspected
Treatment
No disease-specific pharmacotherapy; cholinesterase inhibitors/memantine generally not effective and can occasionally worsen behaviour — not routinely recommended; SSRIs may help disinhibition/compulsions; antipsychotics used cautiously, lowest effective dose, for severe agitation/psychosis; structured environment and carer education central; genetic counselling for familial cases
Prognosis
Relentlessly progressive; younger onset means a long disease course with major impact on family/occupation; median survival from onset ≈6–9 years, shorter (≈2–3y) with MND/ALS overlap; early loss of insight drives high carer burden and earlier institutionalisation

Frontotemporal dementia — primary progressive aphasia variants

Presentation
Progressive language decline ≥2 years with other domains and function relatively preserved early. Non-fluent: effortful/agrammatic speech, apraxia of speech. Semantic: fluent but empty speech, anomia, loss of word/object meaning. Logopenic: word-finding pauses, impaired sentence repetition (usually AD pathology)
Exam
Non-fluent variant may develop orofacial apraxia, dysphagia, extrapyramidal signs (CBS/PSP overlap); semantic variant may develop bvFTD-like behaviour and prosopagnosia; logopenic variant may develop amnestic/other AD-typical features as it progresses
Investigations
MRI: asymmetric atrophy specific to variant (non-fluent: left inferior frontal/insular; semantic: left anterior temporal; logopenic: left temporoparietal). FDG-PET supportive. CSF/amyloid biomarkers usually positive in logopenic variant (AD pathology), typically negative in non-fluent/semantic (FTLD-tau or TDP-43). Genetic testing as for bvFTD if familial features
Treatment
No disease-specific therapy; speech pathology input critical (communication strategies, augmentative/alternative communication as disease progresses); cholinesterase inhibitors may be considered in the logopenic variant given usual AD pathology (extrapolated evidence); manage secondary behavioural/mood symptoms; carer communication training
Prognosis
Variable by subtype; semantic/non-fluent variants tend to stay relatively language-restricted for several years before other domains involve, then progress to a more generalised dementia/behavioural picture; logopenic variant tends to progress similarly to typical AD once other domains are affected; average survival from onset roughly 7–10 years, shorter with associated CBS/PSP/MND features

Mixed dementia (AD + vascular — most common mixed pathology)

Presentation
Combination of amnestic (AD-type) decline and executive/processing-speed impairment against a background of vascular risk factors/history; presentation often less "clean" than a single-aetiology pattern, sometimes stepwise-on-a-background-of-gradual decline
Exam
Combination of AD signs (parietal, memory) and vascular signs (focal deficits, gait disturbance, pseudobulbar features), in proportion to the relative burden of each pathology
Investigations
MRI shows both hippocampal atrophy AND vascular change; amyloid/tau biomarkers may be positive alongside vascular findings — biomarker positivity does not exclude a vascular contribution
Treatment
Combine AD-specific pharmacotherapy (cholinesterase inhibitors/memantine) with aggressive vascular risk-factor optimisation; addressing modifiable vascular risk may slow the vascular component even though the neurodegenerative component continues
Prognosis
Often more rapid functional decline than pure AD alone owing to combined pathological burden; prognosis generally worse than either pathology in isolation; vascular events cause abrupt step-declines superimposed on a gradual neurodegenerative trajectory

Normal pressure hydrocephalus

Presentation
Classic triad (often incomplete, especially early): gait disturbance (usually first/most prominent), urinary urgency/incontinence, and a subcortical/frontal cognitive pattern (psychomotor slowing, executive dysfunction, apathy) with memory less severely affected than AD
Exam
Gait apraxia ("magnetic"/glued-to-the-floor quality, difficulty initiating gait), normal or mildly increased tone, typically no focal weakness; occasional mild upper motor neurone signs from stretched periventricular tracts
Investigations
MRI/CT: ventriculomegaly disproportionate to sulcal atrophy (Evans' index >0.3), narrowed callosal angle, disproportionately enlarged subarachnoid space (DESH) pattern. Large-volume lumbar puncture ("tap test") or extended lumbar drainage with pre/post gait and cognitive assessment supports diagnosis
Treatment
Ventriculoperitoneal shunt surgery — can produce marked improvement, particularly in gait, if diagnosed and treated early; neurosurgical referral essential; tap-test response imperfectly predicts shunt responsiveness; cognitive/urinary symptoms respond less reliably than gait
Prognosis
One of the few potentially reversible dementia-like syndromes — early recognition and shunting can significantly improve function and quality of life; untreated, follows a progressive course similar to neurodegenerative dementias, and shunt benefit diminishes the longer treatment is delayed; weigh shunt complication risks (infection, drainage, subdural haematoma) in frail patients

Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (sporadic prion disease)

Presentation
Rapidly progressive dementia over weeks to a few months (median age ≈60–65y); early non-specific symptoms (fatigue, anxiety, mood change) followed by rapid cognitive decline, myoclonus, cerebellar and visual disturbance, akinetic mutism late
Exam
Myoclonus (often startle-induced), cerebellar signs (ataxia, dysarthria, nystagmus), pyramidal/extrapyramidal signs, visual field deficit/cortical blindness (Heidenhain variant), akinetic mutism in advanced stage
Investigations
MRI DWI/FLAIR: cortical ribboning and/or basal ganglia (caudate/putamen) hyperintensity. EEG: periodic sharp-wave complexes (sporadic CJD; may be absent early). CSF: RT-QuIC (high sensitivity/specificity) and 14-3-3 protein (less specific). PRNP genetic testing to exclude/confirm familial forms. Definitive diagnosis needs neuropathology — involve a specialist prion surveillance unit
Treatment
No disease-modifying or curative treatment; management is purely supportive/palliative — symptom control (myoclonus with clonazepam/levetiracetam), early palliative care given the rapid trajectory, infection-control precautions for invasive procedures/instruments (prion transmissibility), psychological support for family
Prognosis
Uniformly fatal; median survival from symptom onset typically 4–6 months for sporadic CJD (some variant/familial forms longer, but still rapidly fatal within 1–2 years); rapid, relentless progression to akinetic mutism and death — a stark contrast to the multi-year course of the other types in this table
